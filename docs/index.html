Alaska Senate: from the claim to the count

Trump says Sullivan has won. His arithmetic adds every Sullivan Jr. and Heikes vote to Sullivan. This page tests that arithmetic against the ballots, then asks the only question that matters: does it change who wins? Every signed number is Peltola minus Sullivan: positive is Peltola ahead, negative is Sullivan ahead.

What the counted ballots show. The miss is counted minus expected; negative means the new ballots were better for Sullivan than expected. Absentees, most of what remains, came in 251 votes better for Peltola than projected; the 2024 pattern, which expects late absentees to run about 10 points worse for her, has missed them by 1,930. The lesson of the first post-election-night release. The Division counts absentees district by district. All 4,787 absentees added by 9 Nov came from Anchorage districts HD9–16 and HD18, which had no absentees on election night, so one statewide rate moved with whichever districts happened to be in. Every version from 10 Nov on projects each district from its own counted ballots, and the walk starts from election night under that method. The method and its backtests are on the House count check, step 3.

What moved since the last version (11 Nov CVR). Two things changed. First, the Division published its first ballot report since 9 Nov (12 Nov, 1:46 p.m.). Accepted absentees rose from 47,446 to 54,137, and the unreturned by-mail absentees fell from 15,291 to 10,864, so more of the remaining ballots are now in hand rather than expected at 40%. On the 11 Nov ballots alone that moves the projection +1,795 (as projected) and +1,297 (2024 pattern). Second, the 12 Nov export added 5,729 ballots, all from HD1–6: 4,332 absentee, 1,092 early (HD3) and 305 questioned (HD4). They counted +1,820 for Peltola, 433 short of the projection and 65 short of the 2024 pattern. Re-forecasting the ballots still to count takes off 195 and 201. The CVR (cast vote record) is the state's ballot-by-ballot file: one row per ballot, every ranking intact. The 12 Nov export holds 250,434 ballots. Trump's figures, 3,600 + 4,846 == 8,446, come from the official count, which also holds rural election-day ballots not yet in the CVR. The method is the same. This page uses the CVR so every number traces to a ballot.

Conclusion. The claim rests on two assumptions: every Sullivan Jr. and Heikes voter wanted Sullivan, and the count is finished. Step 2 tests the first against the ballots. Step 3 tests the second. On fraud, the reporting cited here contains no evidence. Heikes reached the ballot through the top-four primary. On 29 June the Alaska Supreme Court affirmed that Sullivan Jr. could stay on the ballot, overturning the Division of Elections' decision to remove him after a Republican Party complaint (ADN, Alaska Beacon). On 1 Nov, US District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's suit, which sought to segregate ballots and delay certification if Sullivan lost. Her 20-page order held that the court lacked jurisdiction: the government had not shown the redressability that Article III standing requires, and its proposed remedy was infeasible and would breach ballot secrecy. She did not rule on whether voters were confused. The Justice Department has appealed to the Ninth Circuit (Alaska Beacon, Reason, CNN). Neither court's own text was available online to link; these accounts are from reporting.

Step 2

Election night: the count, and the most confusion could change it

Two walks over the ballots counted so far. Walk 1 is the count as it stands, with no judgment calls. Walk 2 starts from that count and hands Sullivan every Sullivan Jr. ballot where confusion is possible. The gap between them is the most confusion could have cost him.

Walk 1: the count as it stands

Alaska counts each ballot by its rankings. When a candidate drops out, each ballot moves to that voter's next choice, or stops if the voter ranked no one else. A ballot that reaches the final round for Peltola or Sullivan is a two-way vote. A two-way margin is (Peltola − Sullivan) ÷ two-way votes.

Step 3

The projection: what the uncounted ballots could add

Does any reading of the ballots change who wins? This step answers that. It is a model, not a count. It starts from the count as it stands and adds each type of uncounted ballot, district by district, at the rate of counted ballots of the same type in the same district (step 4). Then it applies the step 2 confusion scenarios to the counted ballots and to the ones we cannot yet see.

Assumptions behind the central projection

  • Each district's uncounted early ballots vote like its counted early ballots, and its uncounted absentees like its counted absentees. The Combined Ballot Report lists accepted ballots by House district, so what remains is known district by district. The Division counts absentees district by district, so the absentees counted so far come from some districts and not others; one statewide rate would move with whichever districts happen to be in. A district with fewer than 100 counted ballots of a type borrows a rate from its election-day or early ballots plus the median gap across districts. The method and its backtests are set out on the House count check, step 3.
  • 40% of the 10,864 unreturned absentees arrive. That figure is assumed, not estimated. The range covers 0% and 100%.
  • 5,395 questioned ballots still to count: the report's 6,998 accepted (2,511 in full and 4,487 in part; a partly accepted ballot still counts for statewide races), less the 1,603 in the 12 Nov CVR. They vote like all counted ballots in their district; the 1,603 counted so far came in at −298 against −285 on that rule.
  • Overseas ballots (HD99: 125 received and 83 unreturned still to count) take the overseas rate. Counted overseas ballots run Peltola +0.92 per ballot.
  • The confusion estimates for unseen absentee ballots rest on 362 counted Sullivan Jr. absentee ballots. That is the thinnest input in the broad and stress scenarios.
  • The range +14,174 to +17,081 moves only the absentee return rate. It is not a confidence interval and does not measure uncertainty in how the late ballots vote.

Step 5

Begich against Peltola, 2024: how a count moves after election night

In 2024, Begich's final-round lead shrank by 6,231 after 10 Nov as the late ballots came in.

Two cautions before reading this section

  • My 2024 tabulation has about 2,000 more ballots in the final round than the official result, a gap not yet traced.
  • Much of 2024's late shift came from rural election-day precincts that reported late. In 2026 those precincts are already counted. So 2024 shows that late ballots can move a result. It does not show that 2026's remaining ballots will move it the same way, and the step 3 projection does not borrow it.
