Alaska House: is Begich's lead safe?

Nobody disputes the count here. The question is simpler: does any reading of the ballots, counted or still to come, put Hill ahead of Begich, and what would it take? Every signed number is Hill minus Begich: positive is Hill ahead, negative is Begich ahead. Hill is an independent endorsed by the Democrats, so he is the Democratic side.

Best guess: Begich by 3,160 to 6,017, centre about 4,600. It moved about 1,000 toward Hill since 11 Nov, mostly because the first ballot report since 9 Nov shows more absentees in hand than the projection had assumed; the 5,729 ballots added since 11 Nov landed within 303 votes of what Model A expected and 52 of Model B (what changed). Fewer ballots now remain, so each point counts for less. To win under Alaska's rules, Hill needs the remaining ballots to run about 9 points better for him than counted ballots of the same type in the same district (sensitivity). If every unreturned absentee arrives, the gap closes to 1,949 under Model A. In 2024, Peltola's late absentees ran about 10 points worse for her than the absentees counted first in the same districts; Model B carries that pattern (step 3).

About three-quarters of Begich's counted lead, 8,431 of 11,166, comes from Hafner ballots that stopped. 9,174 Hafner ballots ranked no one still in the race, and 94.5% of those voters chose Peltola in the Senate final round. Most would have stopped with a Democrat in the race too: in 2024, 37.8% of Hafner's Harris voters stopped, against 47.5% of his Democratic voters now. The gap, about 1,640 ballots, is what the independent label cost Hill. If every stopped ballot had followed its Senate vote, Hill would lead under both models.

Hill's column has its own outside votes. Taking off the Hill voters who chose Sullivan in the Senate final round, the same test the stopped ballots use, leaves Hill ahead by 2,728 under Model A and Begich ahead by 258 under Model B. Taking off every Hill voter outside the Democratic ticket, as the main both-sides row does, leaves Begich ahead by 2,396 to 5,339 (walk 2).

The projection is a model, not a count. It covers about 37,000 ballots not yet counted (step 3). The Division of Elections counts absentees district by district, so the absentees counted so far come from some districts and not others; each district's remaining ballots therefore take that district's own rates. Model B uses this same race in 2024, with Begich as the Republican, comparing ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 with those counted by then in the same district. On the ballots counted so far, Begich leads by 11,166.

The lesson of the first post-election-night release. The Division counts absentees district by district. All 4,787 absentees added by 9 Nov came from HD9–16 and HD18, Anchorage districts with no absentees on election night, and ran Hill +33.44 against +8.22 for the absentees counted before them. One statewide rate per type gave those districts +8.22; the district method, run on election-night data, gave +27.85. So every version from 10 Nov on projects each district from its own counted ballots, and the walk starts from election night under that method. The 2024 backtest of both methods is in step 3.

What moved since the last version (11 Nov CVR). Two things changed. First, the Division published its first ballot report since 9 Nov (12 Nov, 1:46 p.m.). Accepted absentees rose from 47,446 to 54,137, and the unreturned by-mail absentees fell from 15,291 to 10,864, so more of the remaining ballots are now in hand rather than expected at 40%. On the 11 Nov ballots alone that moves the projection +1,342 (Model A) and +863 (Model B). Second, the 12 Nov export added 5,729 ballots, all from HD1–6: 4,332 absentee, 1,092 early (HD3) and 305 questioned (HD4). They landed 303 votes better for Begich than Model A expected and 52 better for Hill than Model B. Re-forecasting the ballots still to count adds +56 and +54. So −4,255 + 1,342 − 303 + 56 == −3,160, and −6,986 + 863 + 52 + 54 == −6,017. The flip shift under Alaska's rules fell from +12.2 to +9.1 points (Model B +20.0 to +17.4): the remaining pool fell from 34,930 to 34,542 two-way votes, and the margin to close fell faster. Step 1

The count as it stands, and where the minor candidates' votes go

This step covers the ballots counted so far. Walk 1 runs the count under Alaska's rules, with no judgment calls. The tables after it follow every Hafner and McDermott ballot to where it ends up and show how the same voters marked the Senate, governor and legislative races.

Walk 1: the count as it stands

Alaska counts each ballot by its rankings. When a candidate drops out, each ballot moves to that voter's next choice, or stops if the voter ranked no one else still in the race. A ballot that reaches the final round for Hill or Begich is a two-way vote. A two-way margin is (Hill − Begich) ÷ two-way votes.

Did the party label stop them?

Hafner ran as the Democrat. Hill ran as an independent with the party's endorsement. Some Democrats may rank only a candidate with a D beside the name. The test needs a control, because many Hafner voters stop even when a Democrat is still in the race. In 2024 Hafner was on the ballot again, and the Democrat still in the race was Peltola.

About 1,640 Democratic Hafner voters stopped beyond the 2024 rate, roughly what the independent label cost Hill. Had every one gone on to him, the counted margin would move from −11,166 to about −9,530. The other 6,400 or so would most likely have stopped anyway. These voters rank one candidate in every race: only 11.5% of the 8,314 one-name Hafner ballots ranked two or more candidates for the Senate, against 44.8% of Hill's first-choice voters.

The other side of the label. An independent might draw Republicans a Democrat cannot. On the same ballots, Hill's first-choice voters who otherwise voted Republican (Dan Sullivan for the Senate and a Republican for governor) number 1,741 of 101,672, or 1.71%. Peltola's who otherwise voted Republican (Begich and a Republican for governor): 2,137 of 121,234, or 1.76%. Kreiss-Tomkins's (Begich and Dan Sullivan): 4,400 of 123,085, or 3.57%. Hill draws Republicans at about the Senate Democrat's rate, slightly below it: (0.0171 − 0.0176) × 101,672 == about 50 voters fewer.

Net, the label costs Hill about 1,639 + 50 == 1,689 votes against a counted lead of 11,166. The tests have limits. The 2024 control is small (2,156 ballots) and had a different field (Howe was still in the race). "Democratic voter" means Harris in 2024 and Peltola plus Kreiss-Tomkins in 2026. The three-way comparison of Republican draws mixes the label with each candidate's own pull. Neither test can see voters who stayed home (claims/label_effect.py, claims/label_effect2.py).

Step 2

The stopped ballots, and the other side of them

Walk 2: if every stopped ballot followed its Senate vote

This walk does not change the count. Alaska's rules count stopped ballots for no one. It asks a narrower question: how much of Begich's lead comes from ballots that stopped, and how many of Hill's voters sit outside the Democratic ticket? Each stopped House ballot takes the side of the same ballot's Senate final-round vote. Then, for the second half, Hill voters outside the Democratic ticket come off. The colours mark each judgment call:

  • To Hill stopped House ballot whose Senate final-round vote was Peltola. Judgment call: the Senate vote stands in for the House preference the voter did not mark.

  • Off Hill Hill final-round voter outside the Democratic ticket. Judgment call: Senate first choice not Peltola, or governor first choice blank, a write-in or an overvote. Step 3

The projection: what the uncounted ballots could add

This step is a model, not a count. It starts from the count as it stands and adds each type of uncounted ballot two ways. Model A: each district's uncounted ballots vote like that district's counted ballots of the same type. Model B: Model A, adjusted by how ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 differed from those counted by then in the same district, in the same race with Begich as the Republican. The best guess is the range between them under Alaska's rules.

Assumptions, and how each was tested

  • Each district's uncounted early ballots vote like its counted early ballots, and its uncounted absentees like its counted absentees (Model A). The Combined Ballot Report lists accepted ballots by House district; subtracting the CVR's count in each district gives what remains (step 5). A district with fewer than 100 counted ballots of a type borrows a rate: early from its election-day rate plus the median early-against-election-day gap across districts, absentee from its early rate plus the median absentee-against-early gap. Five districts borrow an absentee rate (HD30, 36, 37, 39 and 40) and eight an early rate (HD1, 2, 5, 30, 36, 37, 39 and 40).
  • Why by district. The Division counts absentees district by district. The 4,787 absentees added to the CVR between the 4 Nov and 9 Nov exports all came from HD9–16 and HD18, Anchorage districts with no absentees in the earlier export, and ran Hill +33.44. The absentees counted before them ran +8.22. One statewide rate would move with whichever districts happen to be in. Estimated on 4 Nov by this method, those districts' absentees came out at +27.85: 5.6 points short for Hill, against 25.2 points short for a single statewide rate (+8.22). On 2024 the comparison is closer (backtest below). The 4,739 absentees added on 10 Nov came in within 111 votes of the district rates, the 2,660 added on 11 Nov 156 votes better for Hill than their district rates, and the 4,332 added on 12 Nov within 3 votes of them (what changed).
  • 40% of the 10,864 unreturned absentees arrive. An assumed value, not an estimate. 0% and 100% are shown in every table.
  • 5,395 questioned ballots still to count: the report's 6,998 accepted (2,511 in full and 4,487 in part; a partly accepted ballot still counts for statewide races), less the 1,603 in the 12 Nov CVR.   • Overseas ballots take the overseas rate. HD99 has 125 received and 83 unreturned overseas ballots still to count. Counted overseas ballots run Hill +0.53 per ballot.
  • The rural batch reads the same with rural or statewide election-day rates (+799 against +873, step 1).

Notes

  1. Data. All tabulations are my own, on unofficial data. Ballot-level figures come from the 12 Nov US House CVR (export started 12 Nov 6:45 p.m. Alaska time, 250,434 ballots, tabulated with RCTab and checked against Alaska's ranked-choice rules), joined on the same ballot ID to the Senate, governor and legislative races. The 130 overseas ballots carry only federal races; they have no governor vote and are not counted as blank for the governor. The official first-choice count is the Election Summary Report of 4 Nov, 1:09 p.m.; the page uses it only for the rural election-day batch not yet in the CVR. The ballot counts are from the Combined Ballot Report of 12 Nov, 1:46 p.m.
  2. The counted ranked-choice result. The CVR's final round is −12,039. The official count also holds 5,355 election-day first choices the CVR does not (mostly rural precincts; 5,306 election-day ballots of the 4 Nov batch are now in the CVR, and the batch is the remainder). Each first-choice group takes the final-round rate of election-day ballots with the same first choice. That puts 5,016 of the 5,355 into the final round as two-way votes and nets +873, a two-way margin of 873 ÷ 5,016 == 0.1740. So −12,039 + 873 == −11,166.
  3. Two counts of the minor candidates. Hafner had 18,486 first choices and held 19,300 ballots when he was eliminated, after transfers from write-ins and McDermott. The tables follow the 19,300. McDermott had 6,538 first choices and 6,566 at elimination. The Round 3 stop count for McDermott (2,126) is lower than the 2,325 McDermott ballots that end up stopped, because 199 of his ballots passed through Hafner first.
  4. The stopped-ballot readings. Both halves are always applied together. Stopped ballots: 13,727 in all, of which 9,174 were Hafner's when he went out and 4,553 had stopped earlier. Their Senate final-round votes: 10,631 Peltola, 1,514 Sullivan, 1,582 neither. Hill voters outside the Democratic ticket: 8,981 by the first-choice test (the definition in hill_adjustments.py), 4,547 by the final-round test.
  5. Versions. What changed from the last version, and links to earlier versions, are in What changed since election night.
  6. 2024 snapshots are the CVR exports of 10, 12, 13 and 14 Nov 2024 and the final, each run through the same ranked-choice code. My final has about 2,000 more ballots in the final round than the official result, a gap not yet traced. The backtest and Model B use the CVR on both sides, so the gap affects both the same way.
  7. Areas are defined precinct by precinct, so they cover the same places on both legislative maps. Absentee, early and questioned votes are reported by district. Where a district is split between areas, each candidate's votes are divided by that candidate's election-day split.
