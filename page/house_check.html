Alaska House: is Begich's lead safe?

Nobody disputes the count here. The question is simpler: does any reading of the ballots, counted or still to come, put Hill ahead of Begich, and what would it take? Every signed number is Hill minus Begich: positive is Hill ahead, negative is Begich ahead. Hill is an independent endorsed by the Democrats, so he is the Democratic side.

Best guess: Begich by 2,620 to 5,318, centre about 4,000. To win under Alaska's rules, Hill needs the remaining ballots to run about 4 points better for him than counted ballots of the same type in the same district (sensitivity). If every unreturned absentee arrives, the gap closes to 677 under Model A. In 2024, Peltola's late absentees ran about 10 points worse for her than the absentees counted first in the same districts; Model B carries that pattern (step 3).

More than half of Begich's counted lead, 6,877 of 11,860, comes from Hafner ballots that stopped. 7,493 Hafner ballots ranked no one still in the race, and 94.5% of those voters chose Peltola in the Senate final round. Most would have stopped with a Democrat in the race too: in 2024, 37.8% of Hafner's Harris voters stopped, against 47.5% of his Democratic voters now. The gap, about 1,330 ballots, is what the independent label cost Hill. If every stopped ballot had followed its Senate vote, Hill would lead under both models.

Hill's column has its own outside votes. Taking off the Hill voters who chose Sullivan in the Senate final round, the same test the stopped ballots use, still leaves Hill ahead by 147 to 2,972. Taking off every Hill voter outside the Democratic ticket, as the main both-sides row does, leaves Begich ahead by 1,489 to 4,280 (walk 2).

The projection is a model, not a count. It covers about 67,000 ballots not yet counted (step 3). The Division of Elections counts absentees district by district, so the absentees counted so far come from some districts and not others; each district's remaining ballots therefore take that district's own rates. Model B uses this same race in 2024, with Begich as the Republican, comparing ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 with those counted by then in the same district. On the ballots counted so far, Begich leads by 11,860.

Step 1

The count as it stands, and where the minor candidates' votes go

This step covers the ballots counted so far. Walk 1 runs the count under Alaska's rules, with no judgment calls. The tables after it follow every Hafner and McDermott ballot to where it ends up and show how the same voters marked the Senate, governor and legislative races.

Walk 1: the count as it stands

Alaska counts each ballot by its rankings. When a candidate drops out, each ballot moves to that voter's next choice, or stops if the voter ranked no one else still in the race. A ballot that reaches the final round for Hill or Begich is a two-way vote. A two-way margin is (Hill − Begich) ÷ two-way votes.

Did the party label stop them?

The other side of the label. An independent might draw Republicans a Democrat cannot. On the same ballots, Hill's first-choice voters who otherwise voted Republican (Dan Sullivan for the Senate and a Republican for governor) number 1,484 of 84,240, or 1.76%. Peltola's who otherwise voted Republican (Begich and a Republican for governor): 1,583 of 99,972, or 1.58%. Kreiss-Tomkins's (Begich and Dan Sullivan): 3,735 of 102,087, or 3.66%. Hill draws Republicans at about the Senate Democrat's rate: (0.0176 − 0.0158) × 84,240 == about 150 voters more.

Step 3

The projection: what the uncounted ballots could add

This step is a model, not a count. It starts from the count as it stands and adds each type of uncounted ballot two ways. Model A: each district's uncounted ballots vote like that district's counted ballots of the same type. Model B: Model A, adjusted by how ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 differed from those counted by then in the same district, in the same race with Begich as the Republican. The best guess is the range between them under Alaska's rules.

Assumptions, and how each was tested

  • Each district's uncounted early ballots vote like its counted early ballots, and its uncounted absentees like its counted absentees (Model A). The Combined Ballot Report lists accepted ballots by House district; subtracting the CVR's count in each district gives what remains (step 5). A district with fewer than 100 counted ballots of a type borrows a rate: early from its election-day rate plus the median early-against-election-day gap across districts, absentee from its early rate plus the median absentee-against-early gap. Twelve districts borrow an absentee rate (HD1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 29, 30 and 36–40) and nine an early rate.
  • Why by district. The Division counts absentees district by district. The 4,787 absentees added to the CVR between the 4 Nov and 9 Nov exports all came from HD9–16 and HD18, Anchorage districts with no absentees in the earlier export, and ran Hill +33.44. The absentees counted before them ran +8.22. One statewide rate would move with whichever districts happen to be in.
