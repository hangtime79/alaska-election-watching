Alaska House: is Begich's lead safe?

Nobody disputes the count here. The question is simpler: does any reading of the ballots, counted or still to come, put Hill ahead of Begich, and what would it take? Every signed number is Hill minus Begich: positive is Hill ahead, negative is Begich ahead. Hill is an independent endorsed by the Democrats, so he is the Democratic side.

Best guess: Begich by 4,278 to 7,499, centre about 5,900. The 10 Nov count moved it about 1,700 to 2,200 toward Begich: the 16,968 early ballots counted since 9 Nov ran more Republican than the early ballots counted before them in the same districts (what changed). Fewer ballots now remain, so each point counts for less. To win under Alaska's rules, Hill needs the remaining ballots to run about 11 points better for him than counted ballots of the same type in the same district (sensitivity). If every unreturned absentee arrives, the gap closes to 2,393 under Model A. In 2024, Peltola's late absentees ran about 10 points worse for her than the absentees counted first in the same districts; Model B carries that pattern (step 3).

About two-thirds of Begich's counted lead, 7,847 of 11,733, comes from Hafner ballots that stopped. 8,524 Hafner ballots ranked no one still in the race, and 94.7% of those voters chose Peltola in the Senate final round. Most would have stopped with a Democrat in the race too: in 2024, 37.8% of Hafner's Harris voters stopped, against 47.4% of his Democratic voters now. The gap, about 1,510 ballots, is what the independent label cost Hill. If every stopped ballot had followed its Senate vote, Hill would lead under both models.

Hill's column has its own outside votes. Taking off the Hill voters who chose Sullivan in the Senate final round, the same test the stopped ballots use, leaves Hill ahead by 1,501 under Model A and Begich ahead by 1,858 under Model B. Taking off every Hill voter outside the Democratic ticket, as the main both-sides row does, leaves Begich ahead by 3,396 to 6,711 (walk 2).

The projection is a model, not a count. It covers about 42,000 ballots not yet counted (step 3). The Division of Elections counts absentees district by district, so the absentees counted so far come from some districts and not others; each district's remaining ballots therefore take that district's own rates. Model B uses this same race in 2024, with Begich as the Republican, comparing ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 with those counted by then in the same district. On the ballots counted so far, Begich leads by 11,733.

Walk 1: the count as it stands

Alaska counts each ballot by its rankings. When a candidate drops out, each ballot moves to that voter's next choice, or stops if the voter ranked no one else still in the race. A ballot that reaches the final round for Hill or Begich is a two-way vote. A two-way margin is (Hill − Begich) ÷ two-way votes.

Did the party label stop them?

Hafner ran as the Democrat. Hill ran as an independent with the party's endorsement. Some Democrats may rank only a candidate with a D beside the name. The test needs a control, because many Hafner voters stop even when a Democrat is still in the race. In 2024 Hafner was on the ballot again, and the Democrat still in the race was Peltola.

About 1,510 Democratic Hafner voters stopped beyond the 2024 rate, roughly what the independent label cost Hill. Had every one gone on to him, the counted margin would move from −11,733 to about −10,220. The other 6,000 or so would most likely have stopped anyway. These voters rank one candidate in every race: only 11.6% of the 7,711 one-name Hafner ballots ranked two or more candidates for the Senate, against 44.7% of Hill's first-choice voters.

The other side of the label. An independent might draw Republicans a Democrat cannot. On the same ballots, Hill's first-choice voters who otherwise voted Republican (Dan Sullivan for the Senate and a Republican for governor) number 1,621 of 94,597, or 1.71%. Peltola's who otherwise voted Republican (Begich and a Republican for governor): 1,775 of 112,598, or 1.58%. Kreiss-Tomkins's (Begich and Dan Sullivan): 4,114 of 114,807, or 3.58%. Hill draws Republicans at about the Senate Democrat's rate: (0.0171 − 0.0158) × 94,597 == about 130 voters more.

Net, the label costs Hill about 1,509 − 130 == 1,379 votes against a counted lead of 11,733. The tests have limits. The 2024 control is small (2,156 ballots) and had a different field (Howe was still in the race). "Democratic voter" means Harris in 2024 and Peltola plus Kreiss-Tomkins in 2026. The three-way comparison of Republican draws mixes the label with each candidate's own pull. Neither test can see voters who stayed home (claims/label_effect.py, claims/label_effect2.py).

Step 3

The projection: what the uncounted ballots could add

This step is a model, not a count. It starts from the count as it stands and adds each type of uncounted ballot two ways. Model A: each district's uncounted ballots vote like that district's counted ballots of the same type. Model B: Model A, adjusted by how ballots counted after 10 Nov 2024 differed from those counted by then in the same district, in the same race with Begich as the Republican. The best guess is the range between them under Alaska's rules.

Assumptions, and how each was tested

  • Each district's uncounted early ballots vote like its counted early ballots, and its uncounted absentees like its counted absentees (Model A). The Combined Ballot Report lists accepted ballots by House district; subtracting the CVR's count in each district gives what remains (step 5). A district with fewer than 100 counted ballots of a type borrows a rate: early from its election-day rate plus the median early-against-election-day gap across districts, absentee from its early rate plus the median absentee-against-early gap. Ten districts borrow an absentee rate (HD1, 3, 5, 6, 29, 30, 36, 37, 39 and 40) and nine an early rate.
  • Why by district. The Division counts absentees district by district. The 4,787 absentees added to the CVR between the 4 Nov and 9 Nov exports all came from HD9–16 and HD18, Anchorage districts with no absentees in the earlier export, and ran Hill +33.44. The absentees counted before them ran +8.22. One statewide rate would move with whichever districts happen to be in. Estimated on 4 Nov by this method, those districts' absentees came out at +27.85: 5.6 points short for Hill, against 25.2 points short for a single statewide rate (+8.22). On 2024 the comparison is closer (backtest below). The 4,739 absentees added on 10 Nov came in within 111 votes of the district rates (what changed).
  • 40% of the 15,291 unreturned absentees arrive. An assumed value, not an estimate. 0% and 100% are shown in every table.
  • 5,287 questioned ballots still to count: the report's 6,581 accepted (2,307 in full and 4,274 in part; a partly accepted ballot still counts for statewide races), less the 1,298 in the 10 Nov CVR. Each votes like all counted ballots in its district. The 1,298 counted so far came in at −480 against −492 on that rule. In 2024, questioned ballots ran 6.39 points more Republican than everything counted by 10 Nov. That is a Begich-positive scenario, worth −317.
  • Overseas ballots take the overseas rate. HD99 has 71 received and 137 unreturned overseas ballots still to count. Counted overseas ballots run Hill +0.53 per ballot.
  • The rural batch reads the same with rural or statewide election-day rates (+1,778 against +1,811, step 1).
  • Model B assumes 2026's late ballots differ from its early-counted ones as 2024's did, inside each district. The race and the Republican are the same; the Democratic candidate is not.

The 130 overseas ballots carry only federal races; they have no governor vote and are not counted as blank for the governor. The official first-choice count is the Election Summary Report of 4 Nov, 1:09 p.m.; the page uses it only for the rural election-day batch not yet in the CVR. The ballot counts are from the Combined Ballot Report of 9 Nov, 2:09 p.m.
  2. The counted ranked-choice result. The CVR's final round is −13,544. The official count also holds 8,949 election-day first choices the CVR does not (mostly rural precincts; 1,627 election-day ballots of the 4 Nov batch are now in the CVR, and the batch is the remainder). Each first-choice group takes the final-round rate of election-day ballots with the same first choice. That puts 8,388 of the 8,949 into the final round as two-way votes and nets +1,811, a two-way margin of 1,811 ÷ 8,388 == 0.2159. So −13,544 + 1,811 == −11,733.
  3. Two counts of the minor candidates. Hafner had 17,232 first choices and held 18,018 ballots when he was eliminated, after transfers from write-ins and McDermott. The tables follow the 18,018. McDermott had 6,202 first choices and 6,230 at elimination. The Round 3 stop count for McDermott (1,989) is lower than the 2,180 McDermott ballots that end up stopped, because 191 of his ballots passed through Hafner first.
  4. The stopped-ballot readings. Both halves are always applied together. Stopped ballots: 12,741 in all, of which 8,524 were Hafner's when he went out and 4,217 had stopped earlier. Their Senate final-round votes: 9,855 Peltola, 1,444 Sullivan, 1,442 neither. Hill voters outside the Democratic ticket: 8,286 by the first-choice test (the definition in hill_adjustments.py), 4,238 by the final-round test.
  5. Versions. What changed from the last version, and links to earlier versions, are in What changed since election night.
  6. 2024 snapshots are the CVR exports of 10, 12, 13 and 14 Nov 2024 and the final, each run through the same ranked-choice code. My final has about 2,000 more ballots in the final round than the official result, a gap not yet traced. The backtest and Model B use the CVR on both sides, so the gap affects both the same way.
  7. Areas are defined precinct by precinct, so they cover the same places on both legislative maps. Absentee, early and questioned votes are reported by district. Where a district is split between areas, each candidate's votes are divided by that candidate's election-day split.
